## 2. Reviewer assignment and review questions

Do not treat a single clinician’s approval as proof of safety or efficacy. A useful minimum review group is:

- A licensed clinician experienced in compulsive sexual behavior / unwanted sexual behavior and aware of the distinction between impaired control, high libido, and moral incongruence.
- At least two people with relevant lived experience and diverse recovery goals, compensated for review; participation must not require disclosure of graphic or identifying history.
- A privacy/security reviewer for the prototype and study workflow.
- An IP reviewer if any source-derived exercises, branded language, or third-party resource content is proposed.

For each exact card, reviewers answer:

1. Is the purpose plain and non-diagnostic?
2. Is the suggested action appropriate for the stated scenario, or could it plausibly worsen distress, shame, dissociation, rumination, compulsive checking, conflict, or unsafe concealment?
3. Are cautions, skip/stop options, and alternatives visible before use?
4. Does any wording imply a guaranteed result, universal mechanism, diagnosis, abstinence requirement, or moral judgment?
5. Is the wording accessible across different goals, identities, disabilities, cultures, and recovery traditions?
6. Are peer support and professional care distinguished clearly? Are any referral claims current and verifiable?
7. Is every phrase original or licensed? Does the exercise reproduce a known proprietary worksheet or program sequence?
8. Should the card be approved for this narrow usability task, revised, excluded, or escalated for additional expertise?

Record: card ID/version; reviewer role and relevant qualification (do not publish personal details without consent); decision; rationale; required change; reviewer date; unresolved disagreement. A disagreement about a material safety boundary blocks that card from the prototype.

## 3. Study question and hypotheses

### Decision question

Does a transparent selector using only a person’s immediate self-selected situation and preferences improve fit or speed of choosing an acceptable next action versus a well-organized static index containing the exact same candidate cards?

### Null / falsifying result

There is no meaningful improvement in fit or choice time, people prefer browsing, contextual prompts create friction or false authority, or the selector worsens agency, shame/distress, or privacy comprehension. Any of these can defeat the selector thesis.

### Primary outcome

For each synthetic task, two independent reviewers—blind to interface condition and participant identity—rate whether the chosen option is acceptable and appropriate to the task on a 1–7 anchored fit scale. Resolve rubric disagreements before unblinding condition labels.

### Secondary outcomes

- Time from task display to the participant’s first acceptable choice.
- Whether a participant can explain why an option appeared and how to reject/change it.
- Perceived relevance and usability.
- Agency (“I felt free to choose another option or none”).
- Shame/distress before and after each task, with an explicit skip option.
- Privacy comprehension: what is collected, whether anything is saved, and whether anyone is monitoring.
- Completion errors, accessibility problems, confusing language, and adverse reactions.

No outcome is a clinical symptom measure, treatment effect, or behavioral outcome.

## 4. Participants and method

- **Target:** 8–12 adults (18+) who voluntarily self-identify an interest in changing a behavior. No diagnosis required; do not recruit based on a screening score, pornography use, or another person’s moral judgment.
- **Exclude from this first formative study:** minors; people seeking urgent/crisis intervention during contact; people unable to give informed consent; anyone for whom the independent reviewers advise that this low-intensity prototype is unsuitable.
- **Recruitment copy:** describe this as research on two ways of browsing draft self-help options, not treatment or therapy. State that the content relates to unwanted behavior change and may feel personal. Participation is voluntary and can stop at any point without explanation or effect on care/services.
- **Format:** moderated 30–45 minute session. No screen/audio recording by default. Use synthetic scenario cards, a timer, and a coded observation sheet. Participants do not need to describe their personal behavior.
- **Design:** randomized/counterbalanced condition order (A→B or B→A); same scenarios, same cards, same text. Rotate scenario order across participants. Optional matched competitor task only if lawful access is available without unnecessary personal disclosure.
- **No account or follow-up tracking.** No real-time deployment, push messages, diary, or longitudinal retention.

### Synthetic, non-graphic task cards

Only use after reviewer approval. Ask the participant to respond to the fictional situation, not to disclose whether it resembles their life.

1. **Urge:** “In this fictional situation, a person feels a strong pull toward a behavior they have chosen to change and wants one next step for the next few minutes.”
2. **Overwhelm:** “In this fictional situation, a person feels emotionally overwhelmed and wants to decide what might help them get through the next few minutes.”
3. **After a choice:** “In this fictional situation, a person has done something they had intended to change and is deciding whether any reflection or next step would be useful.”
4. **No defined moment:** “In this fictional situation, a person wants to explore options but does not want to name more detail.”

Do not prompt about trauma, sexual acts, partners, consent violations, pornographic material, or diagnostic symptoms. Do not turn a fictional exercise into an interview about personal history.

## 5. Session script (draft for review)

### Opening and consent

“We are testing two ways to find a next-step option. This is not treatment, diagnosis, or crisis support. The exercises are drafts and have not been shown to improve outcomes. You do not need to tell me personal details. You can skip any question, stop, or leave without giving a reason. This prototype does not send your choices anywhere. I will record only coded timing and usability notes, not your name or a personal recovery story. Do you have questions before deciding whether to participate?”

Consent must also cover study purpose, foreseeable discomfort, researcher contact, compensation, data access/retention, withdrawal limits after de-identification, and any applicable ethics process. The script is not itself a substitute for a reviewed consent form.

### Task instruction

“Please use the screen to choose an option you think would be acceptable for this fictional situation. You do not need to complete an exercise. You can choose ‘none,’ browse, or stop. Please think aloud only about the interface, not your personal history.”

### Neutral probes

- “What do you think this screen is asking you to do?”
- “What makes this option seem like a fit or not a fit?”
- “What do you think the prototype knows about you?”
- “How would you change or decline this suggestion?”
- “Would you prefer the options be presented another way?”

Do not ask “Why did you do that?” about personal behavior or invite trauma/sexual history disclosure. If personal details arise, do not probe; remind participants they can keep the scenario fictional.

### Close and adverse-reaction check

Ask: “Did any screen or wording feel blaming, pressuring, confusing, or unexpectedly upsetting?” “Would you like to stop, take a break, or talk about support options?” Offer a no-pressure stop and an independently reviewed, current resource sheet if needed. Do not attempt counseling or promise confidentiality beyond the approved study policy.

## 6. Rating rubrics and thresholds

### Fit scale (1–7)

1. Clearly unsuitable or likely to conflict with the stated task.  
2. Mostly unsuitable; substantial mismatch or concern.  
3. Weak fit; meaningful caveat or alternative likely needed.  
4. Possibly acceptable, but participant/reviewer preference is uncertain.  
5. Reasonably appropriate, with minor limitations.  
6. Good fit; clear, feasible, and consistent with the task.  
7. Strong fit; especially clear and feasible, with choice and boundaries intact.

This scale measures scenario fit only. It is not a clinical appropriateness certification.

### Decision gates (formative, not inferential)

- **Advance to a larger comparative usability study** only if median fit is at least 1 point higher on the 7-point scale or median choice time is at least 20% lower in guided mode, with no meaningful worsening on the other primary measure; at least 83% can explain the rule and their ability to reject; no serious adverse event or repeated shame/distress/false-monitoring pattern occurs; and all surfaced cards are considered acceptable for the tested synthetic scenario by reviewers.
- **Pivot to static toolkit/directory** if thresholds are not met, people prefer browsing, or a human-support route dominates as the desired next action.
- **Stop** if any serious adverse event occurs, reviewers identify unresolved harmful or coercive wording, participants commonly believe the prototype is monitoring/providing care, or the privacy model cannot be explained accurately.

At n=8–12 these findings are directional only. Report counts and individual condition differences; do not use p-values to imply efficacy. Record negative and null results.

## 7. Adverse reaction and stop plan

Before any session, identify a named study lead responsible for monitoring participant comfort and a second person available for escalation if needed. Reviewers must approve the precise support/referral sheet for the study location.

- Pause immediately if a participant appears distressed, expresses shame or pressure, or asks to stop.
- Offer to close the prototype, take a break, or end the session. Do not ask for further explanation.
- If immediate danger is disclosed, follow the preapproved local safety protocol; do not imply the product or researcher is continuously monitoring.
- Document the event without graphic or identifying details, using the approved incident process.
- Suspend remaining sessions after any serious adverse event pending independent review.
- Do not resume until corrective action is documented and approved.

## 8. Privacy and security threat model

| Asset / surface | Threat | Minimum control before test | Residual risk / verification |
|---|---|---|---|
| User-entered choices | A sensitive scenario or choice is retained or exposed | No free text; no account; no database; no local/session storage; only in-memory selections | Browser memory, crash capture, OS process state, or a shared screen/device may still expose visible content. Verify code and run a data-flow check. |
| Research notes | Identity or sensitive detail is written into notes | Participant codes; no names in task notes; no graphic details; no audio/video; separate consent/contact/compensation record | Researcher may inadvertently record a disclosure. Train moderator and audit notes before retention. |
| Browser and device | Page title, file path, recent tabs, screenshots, notifications, backup, clipboard | Neutral page title; no notifications/clipboard; use study-controlled device where possible; close browser after session; avoid screenshots | Browser history and shared-device traces may persist. Explain in consent; do not promise invisibility. A standalone HTML file can still leave OS/browser traces. |
| Network | Third-party analytics, remote fonts, image requests, accidental beacon | No imports/assets; no fetch/XHR/beacon/WebSocket; inspect network panel and source before study; offline mode when practical | Hosting platform/server logs may exist if served remotely. Prefer local controlled delivery; document unavoidable logs. |
| Selection logic | Rule reveals or infers sensitive profile | Only current user-selected categories; deterministic, visible rules; no history or inferred attributes | A chosen category is still sensitive in context. Do not store or transmit it. Test explanation comprehension. |
| Research operations | Unauthorized access, retention, accidental sharing | Least-privilege access; encrypted approved storage; defined retention/deletion; no raw screen capture; incident plan | Specific storage, legal duties, and institutional requirements need reviewer determination. Do not assume HIPAA applies or does not apply. |
| Support links | Broken, misleading, or location-inappropriate referral | No live links in this prototype; if later added, verify owner, scope, cost, geography, accessibility, and review date | External sites have their own tracking and privacy terms. Warn before opening them. |
| Misinterpretation | Users infer diagnosis, treatment, monitoring, or guaranteed outcome | Visible prototype-only and not-monitored notices; no efficacy language; facilitator script; easy exit | Some users may still misread a polished interface. Test understanding and stop if confusion repeats. |

**Privacy claim boundary:** “No data is collected” is too broad. The accurate demo statement is that the page code does not intentionally transmit or persist selector choices; visible content and browser/device traces may remain, and research notes are handled separately under the study process.

## 9. Direct competitor teardown protocol

Run only where access is lawful and does not require invented histories, disclosure to a peer group, or unnecessary sensitive account data. Capture factual observations, not screenshots containing private information.

### Access probe completed 2026-10-11

Following Relay’s public “Join” link returned a text-rendered onboarding shell with a headline and an embedded analytics iframe, but no inspectable onboarding controls or product flow. This is an access limitation of the public text view, **not** a direct app walkthrough and not evidence that Relay lacks any feature. No account was created and no user data was entered. Continue with an actual walkthrough only if it can be done lawfully without unnecessary sensitive disclosure.

For each product, use the same non-graphic job story and record:

1. Entry path, number of screens/actions, and elapsed time to an actionable option.
2. What context is asked for; whether free text or identity is required; can the user skip?
3. What is recommended and why, as seen by the user; can it be rejected or replaced?
4. Whether a human-support route exists; timing, conditions, cost, and who sees the user’s activity.
5. Paywall/trial behavior and the point at which payment details or account creation are required.
6. User-visible privacy controls, export/deletion, notification defaults, and sharing.
7. Any accessibility blocker or wording that may imply diagnosis, shame, universal goals, or guaranteed results.

Evidence labels for each finding:

- **Observed:** directly seen during a dated walkthrough; record device/app version and conditions.
- **Publisher claim:** official site, listing, or policy language; quote only short fragments and link the source.
- **Unknown:** inaccessible or not tested; never translate unknown into absent.
- **Interpretation:** researcher judgment, with rationale separated from observed fact.

Do not join or post in a recovery community for research without explicit organization permission, appropriate ethics review, and clear researcher disclosure. Do not use fake personal stories.

## 10. Data plan and reporting template

Collect only: participant code; age eligibility yes/no; consent yes/no; condition order; synthetic task ID; card chosen/none; elapsed seconds; blinded fit rating; brief interface observations; optional distress response; stop/adverse event yes/no. Avoid exact age, diagnosis, sexual history, partner details, religious identity, location, or free-text journaling unless reviewers establish a necessary, proportionate reason.

Predefine a retention period and deletion owner before collection. Keep consent/compensation records separate from usability data. Publish counts, ranges/medians, nulls, adverse events, protocol deviations, and limitations. Do not identify participants in reports or use quotes without separate consent and privacy review.

For every result state:

- What was directly observed?
- What was self-reported?
- What was reviewer-rated?
- What remains unknown?
- Did the result change the product decision?
- What alternative explanations remain?
